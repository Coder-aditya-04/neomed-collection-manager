Why this exists

Marg tells you what is owed. It cannot tell you what is late, because it holds no credit terms — its Due Date column simply repeats the Bill Date on almost every row. So the only thing the export can honestly say is how old a bill is.

Old and late are not the same thing. A hospital that submits bills by the 5th and is paid on the 25th of the following month will routinely carry a 78-day-old bill that is perfectly on time. Chasing them for it wastes the call and damages the relationship.

This system keeps the two ideas apart. Everything follows from that one distinction, including the places where it refuses to answer.

What the file itself revealed

Two things in the real export are worth knowing, because both would quietly produce wrong figures if handled carelessly.

The party totals and the bill rows disagree. For 46 parties, Marg's own header total does not match the sum of the bills beneath it — by about ₹1 crore in aggregate. Adding up bill rows gives ₹8.93 Cr where Marg shows ₹7.93 Cr. This system takes the header figure, as Marg does, and lists every disagreement so nothing is hidden.

Marg already holds a due date for 19 parties, implying credit periods of 60 or 30 days. Those are shown for review but deliberately not applied — a term becomes real only when a person approves it.

Getting started

  1. Open the site and sign in. Each person gets their own login. What they can change depends on their role.
  2. Fill in credit terms. This is the one thing only Neomed can supply, and until it is done the system can show balances but not lateness. Go to Credit master — parties are listed largest first, so the work is finite: the top hundred rows cover most of the money.
  3. Import the file each morning. Export the outstanding bill-wise report from Marg, drop it into Import, read the preview, confirm. About a minute.
  4. Work the call list. Today shows who to call and why. Log what happened as you go.

The system gets better every day you use it. Marg exports no receipts, so payment history is worked out by comparing today's file against yesterday's — a balance that fell is money that arrived. One file tells it nothing about behaviour. A month of files tells it a great deal.

The screens

Today

The current position, and what to do about it. Four headline figures, the portfolio ageing strip, the priority call list with a written reason for each party, and where the money is concentrated.

The Work this first list is ranked by money at risk — the amount, weighted by how far past term it is, weighted again by how that party usually behaves. Each row explains itself in a sentence built from that party's own figures.

Deliberately left off the call list
  • Held by an open claim. The next action is ours, not theirs. They are not defaulters.
  • In dispute, legal, or written off. Handled elsewhere.
  • No credit term recorded. Nothing can be called late without one.
  • Below the small-balance threshold. Chasing costs more than the money.
  • In credit or at zero. They owe nothing.

Parties

All 819, searchable and sortable on any column. Filters for the slices you actually want: critical, above ₹5 L, term not set, credit balance. Clicking a row opens a drawer over the list rather than navigating away, so you keep your place, your filter and your scroll position.

The drawer shows the ageing split, every open bill, and — where the header and bill rows disagree — both figures side by side with a note saying which one is being used.

Import

Drag the file in. It is read in your browser and you see exactly what it contains before anything is saved: the totals, what changed since the previous file, and every discrepancy found. Nothing is written until you press confirm, so a bad export can be discarded without leaving a trace.

Follow-ups, Promises, Claims

Follow-ups lists what was missed first, then what is planned, then what the team has been doing. Claims groups by who owes the internal action, because that is what unblocks the money.

Promises is the one worth explaining. A promise closes only when a payment supports it. Nobody can mark one kept by hand — press Check against payments and the system matches each promise against money that actually arrived, and records which payments justify the result.

Settings

Team and roles, the small-balance threshold, the category defaults, and the full import history with the warnings each file raised.

What it will not do

There is no language model behind this. Questions are matched against a fixed set of patterns and every figure is computed by the database. Three consequences, all deliberate:

  • The same question always gives the same answer.
  • It cannot invent a number. Every figure is read, never generated.
  • It says when it does not understand rather than guessing. A confident answer to a question nobody asked is worse than admitting the miss.

Asked something it cannot honestly answer, it stops and says so.

Ask how overdue a party is when no term is recorded and it will tell you it cannot answer that, explain that bill age is not lateness, give you the figures it does have, and ask for the term. It will never fill the gap with an estimate. This is the single most important behaviour in the product.

Reading the ageing strip

Within terms 1–30 days over 31–60 over 60+ over

Every bucket is measured against that party's own expected due date, never against raw bill age. The four segments always add up to the figure Marg shows, even where the bill rows disagree with the header.

A hatched grey strip means no credit term is recorded. It is not an empty strip and it is not zero — it means the question cannot be answered yet. Money in credit is marked in violet; it is an advance, not a debt.

The rules it will not break

These are enforced by the database, not by the interface. They hold even if someone works around the screens.

Rule
1A credit term is never guessed. Not from bill dates, not from due dates, not from past behaviour. If it is not recorded, the system says so and asks.
2A category default is not an approved term. Where one is used it is labelled an assumption everywhere it appears.
3A party's total comes from Marg's own header figure, never from adding up bill rows.
4Imported rows are never edited. Correcting a figure means importing a new file. Marg stays the financial truth.
5A promise closes only when a payment supports it. Nobody can mark one kept by hand.
6A credit balance is never counted as debt. 97 parties are in credit by ₹12.61 L between them.
7Money reads the Indian way — ₹89.72 L, ₹7.93 Cr — and figures line up in columns.
8Every figure traces to a snapshot and a rule. No estimates, and no rounding that hides a discrepancy.

Two things are true on day one, and both are expected.

The book cannot be aged yet. No credit term has been recorded, so all ₹8.06 Cr sits under "cannot be judged". Typing in the terms for the largest parties is what turns this from a list of balances into a collection system.

Payment history is empty. It is derived by comparing consecutive exports, and only one file has been imported. Import tomorrow's and it begins; expect a few weeks before behaviour ratings mean much.

Honest limitations

  • The export is manual. If nobody exports the file, the book goes stale. Every screen shows the age of the snapshot it is reading for exactly that reason.
  • Payment history is inferred, not read. Marg exports no receipts, so payments are derived from balances falling between two files. Reliable, but money paid and re-billed the same day is invisible.
  • Behaviour ratings need history. A party stays unrated until three of its bills have been settled and observed.
  • Some bill series are unclassified. 1,634 rows use voucher prefixes (CRA, CRB, CRC, CRD) that were not in the original specification. They are imported and counted correctly, but grouped as "other" until someone at Neomed says what those series mean.
